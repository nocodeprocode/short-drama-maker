# AI Micro Drama Writer

Write vertical micro dramas that a video model can actually produce, at the standard the apps commission. This encodes working industry practice for the format, rewritten for a production pipeline where the camera, the cast, and the crew are all one model.

The genre rules come from live-action practice and are non-negotiable, because the audience does not care how the show was made. The production rules are specific to generation. Where the two conflict, this skill says which one wins.

**This repo's source of truth is section 22 and the engine** (`src/drama-engine`, `src/engine`). Sections 1–11 encode live-action / generic-AI method: 4–8s singles, 15–25 chopped shots, VO as a third of the show, on-screen labels as writing. That method was produced here and rejected. For Short Drama Maker, do not write 4–8s singles or VO-led episodes. The paid SKU is 60–90s of 4–6 continuous **scene takes** (10–15s each, 5–8 spoken cues, native Seedance speech, FaceTime-close or closer). VO is not wired. If a bullet above section 22 fights the engine, the engine wins.

## 0. What you are actually making

**A micro drama is an advertisement for coins.**

The apps commissioning these came out of mobile gaming, not film or YouTube. Their business is selling coin packs. The show is the mechanism that gets a coin pack bought. A live-action hit can turn $30M in transactions against a $200K production, with roughly $28M of that going straight back out as ad spend. Production is the cheap part. The show exists to make the ad spend convert.

Some apps want around 80% return on ad dollars, against a typical social ad benchmark closer to 1%. A viewer has to see an ad, watch it through, tap out, install, load money, and pay. Five points of friction, all of them paid for by the writing.

**AI changes one number in that equation and nothing else.** Production cost falls toward zero. Ad spend does not. So the marketing burden on the script goes up, not down: when production is nearly free, the script is the only thing left that can move conversion. Write like an ad copywriter, not a novelist. Agency copywriting is the most useful prior training for this work. Playwriting and prestige TV instincts actively hurt and must be switched off deliberately.

**What AI genuinely buys you** is not cheaper episodes. It is iteration. You can write five different opening hooks, generate all five, run them as ads, and keep the one that converts. Live action cannot do that. Section 15 covers how to write for it.

## 1. What changes when the camera is a model

Four things, and they change what you write, not just how it gets made.

1. **The generation unit is now a writing unit.** Generic AI method: 4–8s singles, 15–25 per episode. **This repo:** 10–15s scene takes with 3–4 numbered shots inside each clip. An episode is 4–6 takes (18–24 used shots). Do not write 4–8s singles for this engine.
2. **Consistency replaces budget as the constraint.** Live-action writers limit locations to save money. You limit locations, wardrobe, and cast to stop faces and rooms from drifting. Same discipline, different reason, tighter enforcement.
3. **Some things are simply unavailable.** Crowds, walking, fighting, hands doing tasks, readable text in frame. These are not expensive, they are broken. Write around them permanently.
4. **Voice over is the safest generic-AI channel — and it is not wired on this SKU.** Industry method uses VO as a third of the show. This engine speaks native Seedance only. Write interiority as a spoken line or a parenthetical tell. Do not plan VO as a third of the episode.

## 2. Hard specs

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Episode length | 60 to 120 seconds, 90 is the working average |
| Aspect ratio | 9:16 vertical, generated native, never cropped from 16:9 |
| Scene takes per episode (this repo) | 4 to 6 continuous takes |
| Take length (this repo) | 10 to 15 seconds generated, used in full |
| Internal shots per take (this repo) | 3 to 4 numbered shots the model cuts itself |
| Legacy chopped singles (do not write here) | 15 to 25 rows of 4 to 8 seconds |
| Opening hook | Within the first 3 to 5 seconds |
| End hook | Every episode, without exception |
| Loop | 3 to 7 episodes forming a self-contained arc |
| Loops per series | 6 to 10 |
| Episodes per series | 40 to 60 |
| Total runtime | Roughly feature length |
| Release | Whole series at once, not weekly |
| Free block | Around 10 episodes, then a paywall (sometimes several) |

**Critical framing correction.** A series is feature length but is **not a feature film cut into chunks, and not a TV show cut into chunks.** Different pacing, different mechanisms, different visual language, different writing. Anyone who writes two hours and slices it every 90 seconds produces something unwatchable. Build from 90 second units at the outline stage.

## 3. The unit hierarchy

```
SERIES  (40-60 episodes, one core expectation, closed ending)
  └─ LOOP  (3-7 episodes, self-contained arc, ends on a pleasure point)
       └─ EPISODE  (60-120s, one escalation, ends on a CPI moment)
            └─ BEAT  (a change in power, knowledge, or presence)
                 └─ SCENE TAKE  (10–15s, 5–8 cues, 3–4 internal shots)
                      └─ SHOT  (the cut inside the take; one sentence per cue)
```

Write top down. Core expectation, then loops, then episodes, then beats, then shots. Never start at the line level and never start at the prompt level.

## 4. Loops

A loop is 3 to 7 consecutive episodes telling the story in microcosm. Roughly an act, closer to a self-contained short story.

**The loop test.** A viewer joining blind at the start of a loop should, by the end of it, know who these people are, what they are to each other, and get a payoff: the villain takes a hit, the protagonist gains ground. That payoff is the **pleasure point**. Every loop needs one.

**Why loops exist commercially.** The apps advertise by running a whole episode, or a whole loop, as the ad. A viewer who just gave ten minutes to an ad follows it to the app rather than write off the time. The loop must therefore work standalone with zero prior context.

**Consequences for your writing:**

- Open every loop with a fresh orienting hook, not just episode 1. Some viewers enter here cold.
- Re-establish relationships inside the first episode of each loop, through conflict, never through recap.
- End each loop on a pleasure point, then immediately open a bigger question.
- The pleasure point is a **partial** win. Nothing closes until the last loop.

**Loops are also your generation batch.** Lock wardrobe, lighting, and location set per loop and hold them for its whole run. A wardrobe change is a consistency reset, so spend it at a loop boundary where the story is turning anyway.

**Standard 8-loop spine for a 50 episode series:**

| Loop | Episodes | Function | Pleasure point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1-6 | Injustice established, secret planted, leads meet | Protagonist lands one small public counterpunch |
| 2 | 7-12 | The trap tightens, paywall lands inside here | One ally realizes the truth |
| 3 | 13-19 | A rival power enters | A humiliation is repaid in kind |
| 4 | 20-26 | The secret partially surfaces | Antagonist loses control of a resource |
| 5 | 27-33 | Betrayal from inside the protagonist's camp | The betrayer is exposed |
| 6 | 34-40 | Public reveal | Mirror scene: the loop 1 humiliation replayed, inverted |
| 7 | 41-46 | Antagonist's last and worst play | Protagonist survives it on their own terms |
| 8 | 47-52 | Collapse and settle | Full resolution, one door left open |

## 5. The core expectation

Every series is built on one question the viewer expects it to answer. State it in a single sentence before writing anything.

- When will he realize she is the woman he destroyed?
- When will they find out they are the parents of the same child?
- When will she stop pretending and take the company back?

The core expectation is the promise. The plot is the delay mechanism. They are not the same thing and confusing them is how these shows die at episode 14.

**Two rules follow:**

1. **Episode 1 must contain the kernels of the entire development.** Everything the series will eventually pay off is visible in some form inside the first 90 to 120 seconds.
2. **If it is a love story, and about 99% are, the leads meet in episode 1.** Ideally within five seconds. If they have not met by the end of episode 1, the show is broken.

A reliable opening move: start at a late, high-heat moment (the wedding, the confrontation, the bedroom) and flash back. Starting at the temperature you want and explaining it afterward always beats building to it.

## 6. Opening hooks

**Come in at the height of the conflict.** If the show is about a man and his wife fighting, the fight is the first thing on screen. Not the drive home, not the door, not the first cold remark.

**Cut exposition entirely.** There is no runway. A streaming drama spends ten minutes seating you. You have three seconds, and this viewer has not bought a ticket. They are deciding whether to.

**The hook is the marriage of the first image and the first line.** Not either alone. The image sets tone because it lands first, the line supplies tension. If 30 seconds pass with no dialogue, something is wrong.

**The mechanism is dissonance.** Put an image and a line together that do not belong together. The gap is the hook.

Worked example from a produced werewolf series: opening image is an opulent castle interior, a corridor of women in robes, wealthy, ceremonial, possibly about to be sacrificed. Over it, the lead says flatly, in voice over, that she does not know what she is doing here and has never even met a wolf. Rich ominous image plus an ordinary confession about werewolves. That mismatch buys the next ten seconds.

**Generation note.** That example is also the ideal AI opening, and not by accident: a static atmospheric wide with no faces to keep consistent, carried by voice over. Atmosphere generates clean. Write hooks that put the burden on the line and the mood rather than on a performance.

**Hook recipe:**

1. Pick a high-status or high-threat image (castle, boardroom, hospital, wedding, bed).
2. Write a line that is flat and ordinary against it, or accusatory and specific.
3. The viewer should be missing information, never missing orientation. Confusion loses them. Curiosity keeps them.

**You do not need a maximum-force hook on every episode.** Once the show is working it carries the viewer forward. Spend hook effort on episode 1 and the first episode of every loop.

**The real target is the 30 second mark.** The opening buys ten seconds, then the next ten, until leaving feels like a loss.

## 7. End hooks: the CPI moment

**The end hook matters more than the opening hook.** The industry term is the **CPI moment**, from cost per install. It is where the viewer decides to continue, and therefore where the money is.

It does not have to be a huge cliffhanger. It has to be one of:

1. **A cliffhanger.** A consequence begins and stops mid-motion.
2. **An emotional gut punch.** No new plot, just a devastation landing.
3. **A lingering question.** Something is now unanswerable and unbearable.

**The governing rule: increase tension, never resolve it.**

This is why drama suits the format and comedy struggles. Structurally, drama increases tension and comedy resolves it. A joke lands, pressure drops, phone goes down. Comedy is possible here but must be built as drama with funny surfaces, and the episode still ends tense.

**Craft rules:**

- End on a consequence starting, not a question being asked. "She dials the number she swore she never would" beats "What will she do now?"
- Cut slightly earlier than feels comfortable.
- Never end on a character leaving. Movement away from camera reads as closure.
- Rotate the type across the series: arrival, revealed object, overheard line, power declaration, physical stop, deadline set. Ten episodes of one type trains the viewer to predict the ending and kills the tap.
- The strongest CPI moment in the series goes on the last free episode. That cut has to survive a payment screen.

**Generation note.** Four of the six types are cheap and reliable: revealed object (a static push on a prop), overheard line (voice over plus a reaction), power declaration (one face, one line), deadline set (a clock, a phone screen composited in post). Arrival and physical stop involve motion and contact, which are the risky ones. Write those two as reaction shots: cut to the face receiving the arrival rather than generating the arrival itself. Withholding the arriving figure is better writing anyway.

## 8. Dialogue

Everything you were taught about subtext is inverted here.

**Write casual spoken English.** Simple, contracted, implied. Not a lawyer, not a prestige script. If she cannot go to the hospital she says "I can't go there" or "If they see me I'm done" — not "Do not bring me to the hospital. It is not something that I can do." Clarity still beats craft. Implication beats restating the same no three times.

**Voice over is standard in the live-action genre. It is not on this SKU.** Industry method uses VO as a third of the show. This engine has not wired a VO lane on scene takes. All speech is native Seedance. Write interiority as a spoken line or a parenthetical tell. Do not write a third of the episode as VO.

When a VO lane lands later it will be TTS over silent shots. Until then, a VO line in the script will not be heard.

**Introduce characters with on-screen text labels.** A face appears, the screen says who they are: the billionaire heir, the cousin, the ex-wife. Viewers accept it instantly. It is expected in this genre, it saves thirty seconds of scene, and it is composited in the edit so it costs nothing in generation.

**Line-level rules:**

- Under 12 words per line. One breath, one caption.
- **One sentence per spoken shot.** Two sentences in a single generation is where lip sync and face consistency break. This is a hard limit and it is a writing rule, not a post rule.
- Say a name at most once per take, and only when it lands. Name-every-line is robot talk.
- Concrete numbers for stakes: three million, forty-eight hours, one phone call.
- Casual translatable English. Contractions. No idiom, no dialect, no wordplay. These get machine-translated widely.
- One speaker at a time. No overlapping dialogue, ever. The caption track cannot take it and neither can the generator.
- No greetings, no small talk, no goodbyes.

**The signature line type is the put-down that reverses status.** Write them deliberately:
> "You bought that watch with my father's money. Take it off before you speak to me."

## 9. Pacing and what to delete

**There is no filler. None.**

Cut on sight: backstory exposition, establishing shots unless genuinely mandatory, long moments, silences, unspoken beats between characters, anything sitting still.

**The hard rule: it happens in dialogue, or it happens in action, or it does not happen.** There is no third channel. The meaningful look that carries a scene in a good film does not exist here. This is doubly true in generation, where a held silent performance is exactly where a face drifts.

**Beat density.** Four to six beats per 90 second episode, where a beat is a change in power, knowledge, or presence. Count them in the outline. Two beats is a dead episode and shows up as a drop-off.

**On-ramp discipline.** Entry into the story world is brief and simple. Sci-fi or fantasy, drop the viewer in. No lore dumps. World rules get revealed the moment they cause someone pain.

## 10. Writing the vertical frame

**The reference frame is FaceTime, and you are never too far from it.** People watch these shows on a phone. They want to be close to the person. A tiny wide that loses the face is not coverage; it is a different genre.

**Many face shots.** Dirty singles, held close-ups, stacked faces, over-the-shoulder on a face. The cut should feel like you are in the conversation. If you cannot read the eyes, you are too far. A "wide" is still both upper bodies filling the frame.

**The cast is the product.** Part of watching a short drama is *she is beautiful* / *he is beautiful*. Write and generate striking, camera-ready adult faces — clear skin, defined features, eyes you want to stay with. Never average, plain, tired, or character-actor. Beauty is the face and the presence. Clothes stay modest and on. Fictional, 18+ looking, no celebrity likeness.

**Close-ups are frequent, not rare punctuation.** Hold them for the line. Do not make every frame a one-second eye ECU — that is how faces drift — but do not hide the leads in a room shot either.

**Here is the one real conflict between the genre and the tooling.** Models are safest a bit tighter than a full medium, because a looser shot includes hands and more room to drift. Resolve it like this, in this order:

1. Default to FaceTime-close or closer, because that is what the genre looks like and why people stay.
2. Keep a true medium short. Two seconds of medium is safe. Six of a tiny wide is not.
3. Spend the take on faces: OTS, dirty single, held CU, stacked faces.
4. Never solve a consistency problem by pulling back until the beauty is gone. That is how AI short dramas look cheap.

**Opposition is vertical, not horizontal.** In a wide frame you oppose characters left and right. In a tall frame you oppose them top and bottom. Live-action productions handle this by casting male leads a full head taller than female leads, because height is what creates intimidation and romantic dominance in this shape.

**You cannot cast, so you must block.** Models render relative height unreliably. Write power differences as staging that is unambiguous in a single frame:

- One standing, one seated
- One on stairs, one below
- One on the floor, one over them
- One in the foreground low in frame, one behind and higher
- Camera below eye line for power, above for vulnerability

Every confrontation should specify which character occupies the upper frame. If your script does not say, the model will decide, and it will decide differently every shot.

**Two-shots are rare, and that is convenient.** Live-action shoots avoid them because of multi-camera setups. Generation avoids them because two consistent faces in one frame is the hardest thing you can ask for. Write scenes as alternating singles with occasional over-the-shoulder, where the near character is a shoulder and the far character carries the face.

**Location economy.** Three to five locations for a whole series, reused. Reuse is a story asset because it enables mirror scenes late in the run, and a generation asset because a location that has been generated fifty times is a location you control.

## 11. Writing to the shot

Deliver scripts already broken into generation units. Industry method: 15 to 25 rows of 4–8s singles. **This repo:** 4 to 6 scene takes, each with a 3–4 row shot list inside the take. Do not hand the engine 15–25 chopped singles.

| # | Sec | Type | Who | Channel | Line | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | Medium, low angle | Vanessa | Sync | "Get out of my company." | Vanessa upper frame, cold light |
| 2 | 3 | Medium | Elena | Silent | (reaction) | Elena lower frame |
| 3 | 5 | ECU | (prop) | VO | Elena VO: "He signed it in my name." | Push on the document |

**Channel** is the most important column. Every shot is one of three:

- **Sync**: character speaks on camera, needs native model audio and real lip sync. Highest risk. One sentence maximum.
- **VO**: voice over across any image. Lowest risk. Use generously.
- **Silent**: a reaction, an object, atmosphere. Near-zero risk, generates clean on the first attempt.

**Industry target mix: about 40% sync, 30% VO, 30% silent.** If your sync share climbs past half, you have written a stage play and a chopped-single pipeline will punish you. **This repo:** scene takes are spoken. VO is not a mix share. Write 5–8 cues per take. Silent is a parenthetical stun, not a third of the show.

**Reaction shots are the genre's natural rhythm and the model's safest output at the same time.** Line, reaction, counter-line, reaction. Lean on it without guilt: it is what these shows already look like.

**Dialogue must come from the model with native audio and real lip sync.** Text-to-speech laid over silent generated video is the single most recognizable amateur tell in the format. Mouth timing is never right and viewers who cannot articulate why will still leave. Voice consistency per character across episodes matters more than perfect face consistency: a face that shifts slightly reads as a different lens, a voice that shifts reads as a different person.

## 12. Locks

Write these into the series bible before episode 1 and paste them identically into every generation.

**Character lock.** Per character: age, build, hair, eye color, a specific beautiful face (eyes, bone, mouth, skin), and a fixed wardrobe for the current loop. Same words every time. Never paraphrase your own character description between shots. If the face would not stop a thumb on a For You page, rewrite it.

**Wardrobe lock.** One outfit per character per loop. Solid, high-contrast colors. No patterns, no logos, no fine texture, no jewelry that has to match. Give the antagonist and protagonist opposing palettes so a viewer can read the frame instantly at thumbnail size.

**Voice lock.** One voice per character, fixed before any dialogue is generated, held across all 50 episodes.

**Location lock.** Three to five, each with a fixed description and a fixed lighting state. Do not write a location "at night" in one episode and "in the afternoon" in another unless the time change is a story beat.

**Prop discipline.** Any object that must be recognized later gets its own close-up when it is introduced, so it never has to be consistent inside a wider shot.

## 13. What never to write

These are not expensive. They are broken. Design around them permanently.

| Never write | Write instead |
|---|---|
| Crowds, banquets, packed rooms | One foreground table, crowd ambience in sound |
| Walking, running, entering a room | The reaction to the arrival, or a static figure already in the doorway |
| Fights, slaps, physical contact | Three tight shots and a sound cue. Cut at contact |
| Hands doing tasks, passing objects, writing | Hands out of frame, or the object alone in close-up |
| Readable text: documents, phone screens, signs | Write the beat, mark the text as a post composite |
| Costume changes mid-loop | Change wardrobe only at loop boundaries |
| Mirrors, reflections, glass with a face in it | Any other framing |
| Two leads sharing a frame for a long beat | Alternating singles, or an over-the-shoulder |
| Children and animals in performance | Reference them in dialogue, keep them out of shot |
| Driving, vehicles in motion | Parked interior, or exterior sound only |

**The meta-rule: when a beat is risky, make the shot tighter and shorter.** The genre's grammar and the model's limits point the same way, which is the single luckiest fact about producing this format with AI.

## 14. What actually makes people watch

The plot is not the product. Emotion is.

The most accurate description anyone in this industry gives: it is not about the story and not about the plot, it is about the **dramas, plural**. Plot holes and loopholes matter far less than whether the emotion is identifiable, relatable, and cathartic. A viewer forgives a broken plot. They do not forgive a flat feeling.

**When you have to choose, always choose the emotion.** If a plot fix costs you a moment of catharsis, keep the catharsis and let the plot creak.

**This is also the AI-specific quality bar.** The failure mode of AI drama is not bad plotting, it is emotional weightlessness: technically clean shots where nothing is felt. If a generated episode feels hollow, the problem is almost never the render. It is that the writing had no catharsis in it to render.

**The audience is a genre audience.** Think of romance readers who go through two hundred books a year, all broadly the same, differing slightly. The pleasure is the itch scratched with a slightly different shaped finger. Same for spy and military fiction. Your job is not novelty at the premise level. It is a familiar shape, executed with conviction, with one fresh angle.

**Three audience segments:** people who love these and say so, people who love these and will not admit it, and people who hate-watch. All three watch. The first two pay.

**Actors matter more than the apps expected.** Fans attach hard to specific performers, exactly as soap audiences always have. Your equivalent is a locked character with a locked voice and a signature behavior that repeats. Build recognizability deliberately, because you have no star to borrow it from.

## 15. The thing AI actually gives you: iteration

Live action gets one hook. You get as many as you want. Write for that.

**Hook variants.** For episode 1 and for each loop opener, write three to five different first-image-and-first-line pairs against the same episode. They should be genuinely different mechanisms, not three phrasings of one idea. Generate all of them, cut them as ads, run them, keep the winner.

**Loop-as-ad variants.** The loop is the ad unit. Write a loop so its pleasure point can be cut two ways: one ending on the payoff, one ending on the next question. Test which converts.

**Episode regeneration.** Live action cannot reshoot after the data comes in. You can. When drop-off spikes on a specific episode, the fix is usually one of three things: the episode had fewer than four beats, the CPI moment resolved tension instead of increasing it, or the episode repeated the previous episode's end-hook type. Diagnose in that order.

**The trap.** The industry already runs on imitation. Ad-scraping tools show which ads got heavy spend, spend is read as success, and winning elements get copied wholesale. If one hit throws a burrito in someone's face, a month later there are ten burrito shows. AI makes that copying nearly free, which means the copy loop runs faster and convergence to slop is the default outcome. Take the shape that is working, then change the variable that matters, which is emotion, never props.

## 16. Genre and trope selection

**Primary tropes:** flash marriage to a billionaire, hidden identity CEO, revenge return, secret baby, alpha or werewolf mate, rebirth or time loop, substitute bride, contract marriage, mafia protector.

**Secondary tropes, stack two:** amnesia, fake fiance, forced proximity, enemies to lovers, terminal misdiagnosis, switched DNA test, the family who sold her out, wicked stepmother, loyal bodyguard, the will reading.

**Genre and generation.** Some tropes are cheap to generate and some are not. Werewolf and fantasy premises need transformations and creatures, which are risky, so write the transformation as reaction plus sound and never show it directly. Billionaire and boardroom premises are the safest: interiors, seated confrontations, few bodies, natural height staging. Period settings are the most expensive because wardrobe and set detail drift visibly. If the brief is open, choose contemporary interiors.

**Underexploited space:** fake true crime, reality formats, genres outside soap. The format's best practices (short episodes, undeniable end hooks, fast pacing, brief on-ramp) transfer to any genre. Soap is just what arrived first.

**Titles** are genre labels: declarative, 4 to 8 words, trope legible. Names are functional and interchangeable. Do not spend creative energy on either.

## 17. Deliverables

**Series bible**
- Title, logline, primary trope plus two secondaries
- The core expectation, one sentence
- Character locks: description block, wardrobe per loop, voice, on-screen text label
- Location locks: 3 to 5, each with fixed lighting
- The secret, and which loop each character learns it in
- Loop table: number, episode range, function, pleasure point
- Paywall episode and its CPI moment
- Hook variants for episode 1

**Episode outline**
One line per episode, each containing a change in power, knowledge, or presence, plus the end-hook type. If a line has no change in it, delete the episode.

**Episode script**
Timecoded, hook on the first line, beats marked, CPI moment marked, voice over and on-screen text labels called out, followed by the shot table from section 11 with the channel column filled for every row.

**Ad cut**
Name the loop, confirm it passes the loop test standalone, and specify the two ending variants.

## 18. Workflow

1. State the core expectation in one sentence. If the user has not given one, derive it and state it back before writing.
2. Choose the primary trope and two secondaries. Check them against generation cost in section 16.
3. Write the locks. Characters, wardrobe, voices, locations, before any script.
4. Lay out the loop table with a pleasure point per loop.
5. Place the paywall and design its CPI moment first. Everything before it is a ramp toward that one cut.
6. Write episode 1 so it holds the kernels of the whole series and gets the leads on screen together.
7. Write hook variants for episode 1 and each loop opener.
8. Outline every episode as one line containing a change.
9. Write scripts with shot tables and channel assignments.
10. Run the self-check.

## 20. Anti-patterns

1. **Writing a feature and slicing it.** The most common failure. Build in 90 second units.
2. **Building tension slowly.** No runway. Open at the peak.
3. **Subtext.** Say the thing.
4. **Resolving an episode.** A satisfied viewer stops watching.
5. **Comedy that discharges tension.** Funny is fine, the episode still ends tense.
6. **Lore and backstory.** Drop them in.
7. **Ignoring loop boundaries.** A loop that cannot run as a standalone ad leaves the show with no marketing.
8. **Repeating one end-hook type.** The viewer learns to predict and stops tapping.
9. **Side-by-side blocking.** Vertical frame, vertical opposition, and say who is on top.
10. **Writing scenes instead of shots.** Anything handed over without a shot table gets improvised by whoever generates it.
11. **Sync dialogue everywhere.** A stage play in a medium that cannot shoot one.
12. **Text-to-speech over silent video.** The clearest amateur tell there is.
13. **Solving drift by shooting everything tight.** Individually fine shots, collectively wrong show.
14. **Technically clean and emotionally empty.** The real AI failure mode. Render quality never fixes a script with no catharsis in it.
15. **Fixing plot holes at the cost of catharsis.** Wrong trade every time.

## 21. Glossary

- **Loop**: 3 to 7 episodes forming a self-contained arc, and the standard ad unit.
- **Pleasure point**: the payoff ending a loop, usually the villain taking a hit.
- **CPI moment**: the end-of-episode hook that decides whether the viewer continues. From "cost per install."
- **Core expectation**: the one question the viewer expects the series to answer.
- **Beat**: a change in power, knowledge, or presence.
- **Channel**: whether a shot is sync, voice over, or silent. The primary generation risk classifier.
- **On the nose**: dialogue stating feeling and intent directly, with no subtext. The correct register here.
- **Lock**: a fixed description pasted identically into every generation, for a character, wardrobe, voice, or location.
- **Free block**: the roughly ten episodes before the first paywall.
- **Mirror scene**: a late scene reusing an early location and setup with the outcome inverted.
- **Vertical narrative**: the broad category. Micro drama is its current dominant subset, mostly soap opera by genre.

**Source.** Genre and structural practice distilled from a long-form interview with Tom Woodley, a vertical storytelling writer and producer working across micro drama apps including FlareFlow and Shorticle, formerly head of content at the vertical video app Rizzle, on The Binge Session podcast. His terminology is used throughout: loops, pleasure point, CPI moment, core expectation. Generation practice is added on top and marked where the two interact.

## 22. Binding to this repo (`src/drama-engine` + `src/engine`)

The engine is the source of truth. Local shoots and the backend import the same modules; never fork rules into a worker, an edge function, or a script. Constants: `types/micro-drama.ts`. Writer prompts: `craft/prompt-fragments.ts`. Shot list: `craft/shot-list.ts`. Continuity: `types/continuity.ts`. Lint: `lint/validate-plan.ts`. Repair: `lint/repair.ts`. Joins and labels: `engine/media/ffmpeg-mix.ts`.

**Where this repo deliberately deviates from section 2, and why.** The generation unit here is a 10–15s Seedance 2.5 *scene take*, not a 4–8s single. Chopped 4s singles were produced and rejected (`assets/live/ep1-micro`): faces drifted between generations, speech was cut mid-word, and the episode read as a trailer. Seedance holds identity across 3–4 numbered shots inside one clip, so the shot table of section 11 is still delivered — it is written as a shot list *inside* each take (`Shot 1 … Cut to … Shot 2`), and a 6-take episode yields 18–24 used shots. The cut still lands every 2–4 seconds; the model just does the cutting where identity is safest.

**What holds exactly as written above.** Core expectation first. Leads meet in episode 1. Hook in 3s; CPI end that raises tension; end-hook type rotates. 4–6 beats. Casual spoken English, under 12 words, one sentence per cue, concrete numbers, no idiom (`DIALOGUE_MAX_WORDS`, `TALK_RULES`). FaceTime-close or closer, many face shots, never too far from the people (shot list: FaceTime two-shot → OTS / dirty single → held CU → stacked faces). The cast is strikingly beautiful; that is part of why anyone watches. Vertical opposition: `blocking.upper_frame` names who holds the upper frame; the shot list angles the camera a touch below their eye line and a touch above the other's. On-screen intro labels on first appearance (`intro_labels`, burned by the mixer from the character's first description sentence). Locks: character, wardrobe, voice, location, prop — plus a room-geometry lock from the bible's location line and the plate's vision floor plan. Never write crowds, fights, task hands, in-frame text, mirrors, children, animals, driving.

**Nothing story-specific lives in the engine.** Every fallback line, insert object, recap image, and location prompt is derived from the plan, the bible, or the genre playbook (`hookLine`, `cliffLine`, `insertObject`, `propFromLockText`, `synthesizeLongOutline`). Staging (`blocking.staging`, `blocking.anchor`) is written by the planner from the script — a collapsed man is on the ground, a boardroom is seated — never from a template. Any new story is a new bible; no engine file should need a change.

**Season architecture for a 60-minute run** (`types/micro-drama.ts`, fed into every episode prompt by `writeEpisodeUserPrompt`):
- Five movements: hook (E1–3), setup to the paywall (E10), escalation to the midpoint (50%), collapse to all-is-lost (80%), payoff. Each has one mission; the prompt states it.
- Four cliffhanger shapes rotated — revelation, reversal, deadline, intrusion — so no two consecutive episodes end alike. Milestones pinned: paywall and finale on revelation, midpoint on reversal, all-is-lost on deadline (`cliffShapeFor`).
- Loops as the ad unit (`LOOP_SPINE`, `isLoopOpener`). A loop opener re-establishes both leads, their relationship, and who holds power inside take 1, through conflict — lint `LOOP_REANCHOR`. Any seven minutes of the season must stand alone.
- Tag stacks for cast (job / social / contrast), first sentence is the on-screen label (`TAG_STACK_RULE`).
- Name someone at most once per take, core expectation restated in every prompt, nothing resolves before the finale.

**Repo-specific rules that extend this skill.**
- *Presence ledger.* Who is in the room carries across takes. A character enters only on a cue with an entrance beat ("FELIX: (enters from the back door) Boss—") and leaves only on a cue with an exit beat ("COLE: (turns and leaves) Late."). Those are the only legal locomotion. Nobody appears or vanishes between takes; a third person stands one step back, never in a lead's position.
- *Physics.* One waist-high counter, fixed length, both ends visible. People stand on the floor around it; a stool is legal, the counter is not; no torso merges with furniture.
- *Proximity.* Leads within arm's reach, faces a forearm apart. The gap is charged.
- *One private TELL per episode.* After the other lead looks away, a face changes for one beat — written as a parenthetical. Dramatic irony: the audience learns the hidden identity in episode 1, the other lead does not.
- *No dead air.* A take runs as long as its speech plus a breath (10–15s), never padded to the model max. The only legal pause is a parenthetical stunned beat.
- *Joins.* Hard cuts between takes (3-frame xfade). One short white flash (0.22s) into the button take. No dissolves, no dips to black. The mux audit refuses a cut whose duration does not match its takes.
- *Decency.* Clothes stay on. No drugs. Adults only. A normal short drama is fine — close faces, a charged almost-kiss, a glass on the table, a fight in words. Do not write a nude body or anyone using drugs. Do not put the words nude / naked / cocaine in a video prompt.
- *Voice over.* Not yet wired for scene takes (all speech is native Seedance). When it lands it will be a TTS lane over silent shots; until then, write interiority as a spoken line or a tell.
- *Refs.* Both (or all present) locked fronts + room plate + one prop still + each person's wardrobe-state still for that room (gala dress at the gala, work jacket on the route; `wardrobeForScene` matches look to location, `generateWardrobe` derives the stills from the locked face). Never a previous take's video, never a last-frame image. Photoreal unmarked stills often 400; stamped sheets pass.
- *Dialogue pass.* `llm.polishSceneDialogue` rewrites every take after repair: one idea per line, every line gives or takes something, no interrogation loops, numbers stated once never demanded, the hidden identity legible to the audience and invisible to the character. A rewrite that changes the cast, the order, or an entrance/exit is thrown away.
- *Shot list / coverage.* Each take is 3–4 numbered shots that MUST change camera station: both upper bodies FaceTime-close, over-the-shoulder, a dirty single, a held close-up, a one-second eye ECU on a stun, the reverse, stacked faces, a half-step that still keeps faces large, punch in on a doorway. Patterns rotate per take. 180-degree line holds. You are never too far from the faces. Register is a one-million-dollar For You page short drama, not prestige film. Cast look: phone-close beauty (`CAST_LOOK_CLAUSE`, `FACE_DISTANCE_CLAUSE`).
- *Join cut.* A new 10–15s generation never opens on the same size as the last one ended. Lighting (wet ground, lamps, skin) will not match. Shot 1 of take 2+ is a close-up or both faces stacked (`JOIN_CUT_CLAUSE`, `joinOpenFraming`).
- *Seedance speech.* Official syntax only: `{spoken line}`, `(cheap pulse)`, `<whoosh>`. Never dump `NAME: line` into the video prompt — the model will say "Mara says". Attribute by camera-left / camera-right. `craft/seedance-speech.ts`.
- *Talk.* Casual contractions, imply the reason, no refusal loops (`types/talk.ts`). Polish prompt carries the hospital-loop counterexamples.
